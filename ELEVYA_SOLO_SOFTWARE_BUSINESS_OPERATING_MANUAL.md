## Purpose of this manual

This is the complete operating manual for turning your current assets into a functioning one-person software business.

It covers the eight jobs you will repeatedly perform:

1. Build trust.
2. Research companies.
3. Qualify opportunities.
4. Contact decision-makers.
5. Conduct sales conversations.
6. Scope and sell projects.
7. Deliver software.
8. Convert completed work into more revenue.

This manual assumes:

- you are technically capable but early in commercial selling;
- you do not have a sales team, legal team, project manager, or account manager;
- you have limited capital;
- you must keep the operation lean;
- you want direct clients rather than permanent dependence on marketplaces;
- you will use automation for research and administration, but not to impersonate human judgment or violate platform rules;
- you want a system that can begin manually and mature indefinitely.

The objective is not to create the appearance of a large agency. The objective is to become a highly reliable independent technical partner whose work, communication, and evidence make the business increasingly easier to sell.

# Part I — The business operating model

## 1. What business you are building

Your business is not “freelance coding.” It is a controlled service business that sells operational improvement through software.

### Primary positioning

> **Anass Benameur — Independent Software Engineer building operational systems, integrations, and internal tools.**

### Initial field-service positioning

> I help field-service companies connect interventions, technician updates, parts, documents, and invoicing by integrating the tools they already use or building the missing operational layer.

### Commercial progression

```text
Research
→ qualification
→ relevant contact
→ sales conversation
→ paid mapping or bounded pilot
→ delivery
→ acceptance
→ expansion
→ support
→ testimonial
→ referral
→ stronger future sales
```

Each stage must produce an artifact and a decision. You should never keep a prospect or project in an undefined state.

## 5. Maturity model used throughout this guide

Each business function progresses through six levels.

### Level 0 — Unstructured

You act from memory, improvise, and cannot reliably repeat results.

### Level 1 — Minimum viable

You have a checklist, a template, and one clear output.

### Level 2 — Repeatable

You follow the process consistently and collect data.

### Level 3 — Measured

You know conversion rates, time costs, defects, and bottlenecks.

### Level 4 — Optimized

You automate low-risk repetition and improve based on evidence.

### Level 5 — Compounding

Each completed cycle creates assets, referrals, recurring revenue, and stronger positioning.

“Infinite” does not mean unlimited complexity. It means continuous improvement without losing control.

# Guide 1 — Build trust

## 1.1 What trust means in this business

A buyer does not need to believe you are famous. The buyer needs enough confidence to believe:

1. You are a real and accountable person.
2. You understand the type of problem.
3. You can deliver the defined scope.
4. You will communicate honestly.
5. You will not disappear with access, money, or source code.
6. You will not expose their confidential information.
7. The first engagement is small enough to manage risk.

Modern B2B buyers often research independently before speaking to a seller. Your website, case studies, LinkedIn, GitHub, documents, and email identity therefore perform part of the sales process before a call occurs. [S10][S11]

---

## 1.2 Trust architecture

Trust comes from seven layers.

### Layer 1 — Identity

- real name;
- professional photograph;
- location and time zone;
- languages;
- professional email;
- consistent identity across portfolio, LinkedIn, GitHub, documents, and proposals.

### Layer 2 — Relevance

- clear target market;
- clear problem category;
- language matching the buyer’s operations;
- no generic list of every technology you have seen.

### Layer 3 — Evidence

- truthful case studies;
- screenshots;
- architecture diagrams;
- repository evidence where safe;
- working demonstrations;
- explicit project maturity labels;
- limitations stated honestly.

### Layer 4 — Commercial clarity

- defined entry engagement;
- price range or pricing logic;
- deliverables;
- exclusions;
- process;
- response expectations;
- payment milestones.

### Layer 5 — Delivery controls

- written scope;
- staging;
- acceptance criteria;
- documentation;
- source-code handling;
- backups;
- rollback;
- change control.

### Layer 6 — Social proof

- testimonial;
- reference where permitted;
- client-approved case study;
- public review;
- referral.

### Layer 7 — Reputation over time

- useful posts;
- accurate public commentary;
- reliable communication;
- repeated delivery;

## 1.5 Case-study standard

Every case study should contain:

1. **Classification** — client delivery, academic product, prototype, etc.
2. **Context** — what the organization or project needed.
3. **Role** — exactly what you did and who else participated.
4. **Scope** — what was included and excluded.
5. **Constraints** — time, data, environment, team, legacy system.
6. **Approach** — major technical and product decisions.
7. **Evidence** — screenshots, diagrams, tests, deployment records.
8. **Outcome** — only verified results.
9. **Limitations** — what remains unverified or unfinished.
10. **Current status** — live, demo, preproduction, inactive, private, or archived.
11. **Relevance** — how the work relates to the buyer’s problem.

### Evidence hierarchy

From strongest to weakest:

1. Client-confirmed outcome with permission.
2. Production evidence with logs or accepted deployment.
3. Working demonstration with reproducible steps.
4. Source-code evidence and tests.
5. Architecture and design documentation.
6. Personal assertion without evidence.

Never build a major sales claim on level 6.

## 1.6 Trust-building content system

You do not need to become an influencer.

### Initial publishing rhythm

- one useful LinkedIn post per week;
- one deeper case-study or workflow breakdown per month;
- five to ten meaningful comments per week;
- no engagement automation.

### Content categories

1. **Operational insight**

2. **Decision guidance**  
   Example: when to configure, integrate, or build.

3. **Technical evidence**  
   Example: how idempotent callbacks prevent duplicate processing.

4. **Delivery practice**  
   Example: what acceptance criteria should look like.

5. **Lessons and limitations**  
   Example: what you would harden before production.

6. **Workflow teardown**

### Content quality gate

Before publishing, ask:

- Is this useful without hiring me?
- Is the claim accurate?
- Is it relevant to my target buyer?
- Does it show judgment rather than jargon?
- Could I defend it in a client call?
- Does it expose confidential information?

# Guide 2 — Research companies

## 2.1 Purpose

Company research answers four questions:

1. Is this organization real and active?
2. Does it fit the target market?
3. Is there evidence of operational complexity or a trigger?
4. Is there a legitimate and relevant reason to contact someone?

Research does not prove that the company has a problem. It produces a defensible hypothesis to test.

---

## 2.2 Initial research target

For the first campaign:

- geography: France;
- vertical: commercial HVAC and refrigeration;
- organization size: approximately 20–150 employees;
- operation: recurring installation, maintenance, repair, or service interventions;
- buyer roles: managing director, operations/service/technical director, administrative-finance lead, or digital/IT lead where relevant;
- initial wedge: intervention completion to invoice-ready information.

Do not activate multiple verticals until the first sample is measured.

---

## 2.3 Official and primary research sources

### French legal identity

Use:

- Annuaire des Entreprises;
- INSEE SIRENE;
- RNE extract where needed.

The Annuaire des Entreprises aggregates public administrative data, while SIRENE records the identity of French companies and establishments. [S4][S5]

### Commercial and legal events

Use BODACC for:

- registrations;
- sales and transfers;
- collective proceedings;
- other official commercial notices. [S6]

### Public-contract evidence

Use DECP data for awarded public contracts. Treat this as evidence of activity or capability—not automatic proof of an operational pain. [S7]

### Company-controlled sources

- official website;
- legal notices;
- service pages;
- locations;
- careers;
- news;
- customer portal;
- downloadable forms and documents;
- privacy and legal pages;
- published contact information.

### Human and role research

- LinkedIn, manually;
- official team pages;
- conference or association pages;
- press releases;
- job listings;
- professional directories with clear provenance.

### Avoid as final authority

- scraped lead databases without provenance;
- generic AI summaries;
- unverified business directories;
- old cached pages;
- guessed email patterns;
- social profiles without role confirmation.

## 2.5 Evidence grading

Grade each evidence item.

### Grade A — Primary and current

- official registry;
- official company website;
- official job posting;
- official legal notice;
- official public-contract data;
- verified recent statement by the company or person.

### Grade B — Reliable secondary

- reputable trade press;
- professional association;
- conference page;
- partner case study;
- customer page with a current reference.

### Grade C — Weak lead

- unverified directory;
- old social post;
- cached page;
- third-party aggregator;
- AI-generated summary.

### Grade D — Unsupported

- assumption;
- guessed email;
- copied data without provenance;
- ambiguous person-company relationship.

### Rule

A contact-ready dossier requires:

- legal identity based on Grade A evidence;
- company fit supported by at least one Grade A source;
- operational hypothesis supported by at least two relevant facts;
- buyer role supported by Grade A or strong Grade B evidence;
- contact path with recorded provenance.

## 2.6 Research procedure

### Step 1 — Verify legal identity

- confirm legal name;
- confirm active status;
- record SIREN;
- distinguish legal entity from establishment;
- identify group or subsidiary relationships;
- confirm the official domain.

### Step 2 — Confirm field-service activity

Look for:

- maintenance contracts;
- installation and repair;
- service territories;
- technicians;
- emergency service;
- recurring inspection;
- equipment history;
- certificates;
- parts or consumables;
- customer service requests.

### Step 3 — Estimate complexity

Signals include:

- multiple branches;
- several service categories;
- regional coverage;
- public contracts;
- many technician vacancies;
- multiple customer types;
- separate portals/forms;
- evidence of several software systems;
- documentation-heavy work.

### Step 4 — Identify triggers

A trigger increases timing relevance.

Examples:

- hiring technicians or planners;
- opening a new branch;
- acquisition;
- public-contract award;
- management change;
- website or portal launch;
- new compliance requirement;
- explicit software vacancy;
- rapid expansion.

A trigger is not required, but it improves priority.

### Step 5 — Develop one hypothesis

Use:

```text
Observed fact
+
known workflow characteristic
+
uncertainty
=
question worth asking
```

Example:

> The company operates from three branches, recruits technicians, and provides recurring maintenance. Intervention information may therefore pass between field personnel, office coordination, parts records, and invoicing. The unknown is whether that flow is already centralized.

### Step 6 — Identify the buyer role

Choose the role closest to the affected workflow.

| Workflow | Likely role |
|---|---|
| Dispatch/planning | Operations or Service Director |
| Technician documentation | Service, Technical, or Quality Director |
| Billing delay | Finance/Administration lead plus Operations |
| Software integration | IT/Digital lead plus operational owner |
| Company-wide operational change | Managing Director in smaller firms |

### Step 7 — Find a legitimate contact path

Preference order:

1. Published named professional email.
2. Published role-based email.
3. Published company contact form with relevant routing.
4. Published switchboard or professional phone number.
5. Manual LinkedIn contact where appropriate.
6. Generic company email.

A guessed personal email is a lead requiring confirmation, not a ready contact.

### Step 8 — Decide

- Reject.
- Hold for more evidence.
- Qualify.
- Contact-ready.

Never contact merely because data exists.

# Guide 3 — Qualify opportunities

## 3.1 Purpose

Qualification prevents you from spending sales and delivery time on companies that are unlikely to buy, unsuitable to serve, or dangerous to accept.

A company can be an excellent market fit and still be a poor immediate opportunity.

Qualification answers:

1. Is there a real problem?
2. Is the problem important enough to fund?
3. Is the timing credible?
4. Is there a buyer with authority or influence?
5. Can you deliver the work safely and profitably?
6. Is there a legitimate path to a commercial decision?

---

## 3.2 Three qualification stages

### Stage A — Account qualification

Performed before outreach.

Determines whether the company deserves research and contact.

### Stage B — Contact qualification

Determines whether the person and contact route are appropriate.

### Stage C — Opportunity qualification

Performed after a reply or call.

Determines whether a real paid engagement should be pursued.

Do not confuse these stages. Research can support account qualification, but only conversation can confirm pain, timing, authority, and budget.

---

## 3.3 Account qualification scorecard

Score from 0 to 100.

### Fit — 25 points

| Factor | Points |
|---|---:|
| Correct initial vertical | 0–6 |
| Appropriate company size | 0–5 |
| Recurring field-service activity | 0–5 |
| Multiple operational roles/locations | 0–5 |
| Relevant documentation, parts, or billing workflow | 0–4 |

### Complexity evidence — 20 points

| Factor | Points |
|---|---:|
| Multiple branches/territories | 0–4 |
| Technician recruitment or growth | 0–4 |

### Account decisions

| Score | Decision |
|---:|---|
| 80–100 | Priority contact candidate |

### Hard gates

Regardless of score, do not mark contact-ready when:

- legal identity is uncertain;
- official domain is uncertain;
- there is no professional relevance;
- the buyer-person relationship is unverified;
- only a guessed personal email exists;
- the person or company is suppressed;
- the hypothesis is unsupported;
- the company is outside your delivery capability.

A score never overrides a hard gate.

## 3.4 Opportunity qualification after contact

Use the **PROBLEM** framework.

### P — Problem

- What specifically is happening?
- Which workflow is affected?
- Who experiences it?
- Is it a symptom or root problem?

### R — Result required

- What would a useful outcome look like?
- What must become faster, safer, clearer, or more reliable?
- How will acceptance be recognized?

### O — Ownership

- Who owns the workflow?
- Who approves spending?
- Who controls system access?
- Who will accept the result?

### B — Business consequence

- Delay?
- Administrative labor?
- Billing impact?
- Customer impact?
- Compliance risk?
- Growth constraint?

### L — Limits and constraints

- Existing software?
- Security or data restrictions?
- Deadline?
- Integrations?
- Procurement requirements?
- Budget range?

### E — Economics

- Is the cost of the problem meaningful?
- Is funding available?
- Is the expected project size appropriate for the company and your capacity?

### M — Momentum

- Why now?
- What event creates urgency?
- What happens if nothing changes?
- Is there a defined next action and date?

Score each from 0–3.

| Access/data | Unavailable | Uncertain | Likely available | Confirmed |
| Commitment | Passive | Curious | Willing next step | Paid next step ready |

### Decision

- 20–24: strong opportunity;
- 15–19: pursue with clear missing information;
- 10–14: nurture or small discovery only;
- below 10: close or defer.

## 3.6 Disqualification rules

Close or reject when:

- they want substantial unpaid work;
- the problem has no owner;
- no one can approve spending;
- they refuse to define scope but demand a fixed price;
- they require an impossible deadline;
- the project includes unlawful, deceptive, or unethical activity;
- the company will not provide necessary access;
- they expect you to assume unlimited operational liability;
- payment identity is unclear;
- the budget is incompatible with the requirement;
- the relationship shows disrespect, manipulation, or repeated dishonesty;
- they request production changes without backup or rollback;
- they demand client-confidential information from your previous work;
- the project exceeds your competence and no safe partner is available.

Disqualification protects revenue. A bad client can cost more than no client.

# Guide 4 — Contact decision-makers

## 4.1 Purpose

The objective of first contact is not to close a project. It is to earn a relevant reply that confirms or rejects your hypothesis.

Your message should make it easy for the recipient to answer one small question.

---

## 4.2 Legal and platform boundaries

### French B2B email prospecting

CNIL guidance states that professional prospecting may rely on legitimate interest when the solicitation relates to the person’s professional activity. The person must be informed and must be able to object simply and free of charge. [S2]

Operational requirements:

- contact a professional about a professionally relevant subject;
- identify yourself;
- explain the relevance;
- avoid deceptive subject lines;
- provide a simple objection route;
- stop after objection;
- maintain a suppression list used only to respect objections. [S13]

This manual is operational guidance, not a substitute for legal advice.

### LinkedIn

Use manually. LinkedIn’s User Agreement prohibits unauthorized bots and automated access, messaging, and engagement. [S3]

### Transactional email services

Do not assume a transactional provider permits cold outreach. Review the provider’s acceptable-use terms before sending. Use your normal professional mailbox for low-volume manual outreach.

---

## 4.3 Email setup procedure

### Step 1 — Create mailboxes

Recommended:

- `anass@elevya.tech` — personal outbound and client communication;
- `hello@elevya.tech` — portfolio inbound;
- `billing@elevya.tech` — optional after revenue.

### Step 2 — Configure authentication

Use your provider’s exact records for:

- SPF;
- DKIM;
- DMARC.

Do not publish multiple conflicting SPF records.

### Step 3 — Test

Send legitimate messages to:

- Gmail;
- Outlook/Hotmail;
- another professional domain.

Inspect authentication results:

```text
SPF=pass
DKIM=pass
DMARC=pass
```

Google recommends SPF, DKIM, and DMARC and notes that unauthenticated messages may be rejected or treated as spam. [S1]

### Step 4 — Secure the mailbox

- strong unique password;
- MFA;
- recovery information;
- no shared credentials;
- review forwarding rules;
- monitor unexpected login alerts;
- keep devices updated.

### Step 5 — Build normal usage

Use the mailbox for real business communication before and during outreach. Do not use fake “warm-up” networks.

---

## 4.4 Contact-channel hierarchy

### 1. Email

Best when a published professional address exists.

### 2. Company contact form

Useful when it routes to the relevant department. Keep the message concise and professional.

### 3. Professional phone

Useful for routing questions, not aggressive pitching.

Example:

> Bonjour, je cherche la personne qui supervise le processus entre les rapports d’intervention et la préparation de la facturation. Pourriez-vous m’indiquer le bon interlocuteur ?

### 4. LinkedIn

Use for verification, light professional context, and manual contact when appropriate.

### 5. Warm introduction

Strongest channel when the introducer genuinely knows both parties.

---

## 4.5 Message construction system

Use **ECHO**.

### E — Evidence

One verified company fact.

### C — Connection

Explain why that fact relates to a workflow you understand.

### H — Hypothesis

State the possible issue carefully, without pretending to know.

### O — Open question

Ask one small question that can be answered quickly.

### Example

> I saw that your team operates across several service locations and provides recurring maintenance. In companies with that structure, the difficult handoff is often between completed intervention reports, parts information, and invoice preparation. Is that workflow already centralized in one system, or does part of it still move through separate documents and tools?

---

## 4.6 First-email rules

- 70–130 words;
- one recipient;
- one verified fact;
- one hypothesis;
- one question;
- one relevant link at most;
- no attachment;
- no calendar link as the primary request;
- no tracking pixel;
- no fake “Re:” or “Fwd:”;
- no false familiarity;
- no exaggerated compliment;
- no long service list;
- no automatic sequence;
- no claims of guaranteed ROI;
- include a simple opt-out sentence.

French opt-out sentence:

> Si ce sujet n’est pas pertinent, dites-le-moi simplement et je ne vous recontacterai pas.

Use simple, descriptive subjects.

Examples:

- `Organisation des interventions – [Company]`

- `Urgent`

- clickbait.

## 4.11 Reply handling

### Positive

- answer directly;
- ask one or two qualifying questions;
- offer a 15–20-minute call;
- provide two or three time options;
- update the pipeline immediately.

### “Send more information”

Send only:

- one sentence explaining the offer;
- one relevant case study;
- one question about their workflow.

Do not send five PDFs.

### “We already have software”

Respond:

> That may be the correct situation. I do not recommend replacing a system that already covers the workflow. The question is whether any important handoff still requires repeated entry or manual reconciliation. If not, there may be no project to pursue.

### “Not me”

Ask:

> Could you point me to the person who owns this workflow?

### Negative or opt-out

- acknowledge;
- do not argue;
- suppress immediately;
- record reason;
- do not contact another employee if the objection clearly applies to the company.

### Bounce

- record type;
- stop hard-bounce attempts;
- do not cycle through guessed addresses;
- use another published route only when professionally appropriate.

6. Confirm recipient and domain.
7. Confirm only one CTA/question.
8. Send manually.
9. Record timestamp, subject, body version, evidence used, and follow-up date.
10. Check for bounce or reply.

Initial volume:

- 2 new messages per working day;
- 8–10 per week;

# Guide 5 — Conduct sales conversations

## 5.1 Purpose

A sales conversation is not a performance in which you prove how much technology you know. It is a structured investigation that determines whether a useful, fundable, deliverable change exists.

The goal is to create decision clarity for both sides.

---

## 5.2 Types of conversations

### 1. Fit call

- 15–20 minutes;
- verifies relevance;
- identifies workflow and owner;
- determines whether deeper discovery is justified.

### 2. Discovery conversation

- 30–60 minutes;
- maps the current process;
- identifies business impact, constraints, stakeholders, and target result.

### 3. Mapping workshop

- paid;
- 60–120 minutes or several interviews;
- produces the documented current and target workflow.

### 4. Proposal review

- 30–45 minutes;
- confirms scope, assumptions, exclusions, milestones, price, and decision process.

### 5. Project review

- delivery-focused;
- demonstrates progress and resolves decisions;
- not a sales call, though it may expose expansion opportunities.

Do not combine every conversation into an unfocused two-hour meeting.

---

## 5.3 Fit-call preparation checklist

Before the call:

- [ ] Re-read the company dossier.
- [ ] Re-read all messages.
- [ ] Confirm the person’s role.
- [ ] Write your current hypothesis in one sentence.
- [ ] Prepare five essential questions.
- [ ] Prepare one relevant proof asset.
- [ ] Open a notes document.
- [ ] Set a timer.
- [ ] Test microphone, camera, and connection.
- [ ] Close distracting applications.
- [ ] Know your desired next-step options.

Do not prepare a complete technical solution before hearing the actual problem.

---

## 5.4 Fit-call agenda

### Minute 0–2 — Frame the call

Example:

> Thank you for the time. I would like to understand how the workflow operates today, confirm whether the issue I mentioned is real, and decide whether there is a useful next step. I will not try to force a project if the process is already working well.

This reduces pressure and establishes seriousness.

### Minute 2–7 — Context

Ask:

- What made you respond?
- What is your role in this process?
- Which teams are involved?
- What systems currently support it?

### Minute 7–15 — Current problem

Ask:

- Where does information first enter the workflow?
- Where is it re-entered or checked?
- What is commonly missing?
- What creates delay?
- What happens when an exception occurs?

### Minute 15–18 — Importance and timing

Ask:

- Is this a current priority or general interest?
- What happens if nothing changes?
- Who else would be involved in deciding?

### Minute 18–20 — Decide next step

Outcomes:

- no fit;
- send relevant information;
- future follow-up date;
- paid mapping brief;
- direct small pilot when scope is already clear.

---

## 5.5 Discovery-call question bank

Do not ask every question. Select those relevant to the workflow.

### Trigger

- What starts the process?
- Which person, event, or customer request creates the first record?
- Is the trigger predictable or urgent?

### Actors

- Who handles the process first?
- Who works in the field?
- Who validates completion?
- Who prepares the invoice?
- Who resolves exceptions?
- Who needs reporting?

### Systems

- Which software is used?
- Which spreadsheets, documents, messaging tools, or paper forms remain involved?
- Which system is considered the source of truth?
- Which system contains duplicate or conflicting data?
- Are APIs available?

### Data

- What information is captured?
- What is commonly missing or incorrect?
- Which documents, photos, signatures, parts, or certificates are required?
- How long is information retained?
- Is personal or sensitive data involved?

### Flow

- What happens step by step?
- Where is data copied?
- Where does work wait?
- What is approved manually?
- What is sent by email?
- What happens outside the main system?

### Volume

- How many jobs, users, technicians, branches, or documents are involved?
- How does volume vary?
- What happens during peak periods?

### Exceptions

- What happens when a technician cannot complete a job?
- What happens when a part is missing?
- What happens when a report is incomplete?
- What happens when the customer disputes completion?
- What happens when an integration fails?

### Impact

- How much time is spent checking or re-entering information?
- Does the process delay billing?
- Does it create customer complaints?
- Does it create stock, compliance, or reporting risk?
- Does it prevent growth?

### Existing attempts

- What has already been tried?
- Why did it not solve the problem?
- Which existing tool must remain?
- Is there an abandoned internal system?

### Decision

- Who approves the project?
- Who evaluates technical risk?
- Who accepts the result?
- What budget range is plausible?
- What deadline is real?
- What procurement or legal process applies?

### Success

- What must be true for the project to be considered successful?
- Which behavior or metric should change?
- What is the smallest useful outcome?

---

## 5.6 Listening and note-taking method

Use four columns:

| Statement | Evidence/fact | Assumption/question | Commercial implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| “Reports arrive late” | Reports often submitted next morning | How often? Why? | Potential invoice delay |

Separate what the client said from your interpretation.

### Reflective confirmation

Use:

> Let me confirm that I understood correctly…

Then summarize:

- the workflow;
- the main delay;
- the affected people;
- the desired result.

Ask the buyer to correct you.

---

## 5.7 How to speak about technology

Translate technology into operational effects.

| Technical term | Buyer-facing explanation |
|---|---|
| API integration | The two systems exchange information without re-entry |
| Role-based access | Each person sees and changes only what their role requires |
| Idempotency | Repeated callbacks do not create duplicate business actions |
| Audit log | You can see who changed what and when |
| Queue/retry | Temporary external failures do not immediately lose the work |
| Staging | The team can review the system safely before production |
| Automated tests | Important behavior is checked before each release |
| Backup/rollback | There is a defined recovery path if deployment fails |

Do not remove technical detail when it matters. Explain why it matters.

---

## 5.8 Objection handling

Objections are information. Do not respond defensively.

### “You are based outside France”

Response structure:

1. acknowledge;
2. explain operational controls;
3. reduce initial risk.

Example:

> That is a reasonable concern. I work through written scope, scheduled demonstrations, staging, documented acceptance, and milestone payments. The first engagement can remain small and bounded so that you evaluate delivery before making a larger commitment.

### “We already have software”

> That may be correct. I do not recommend replacing a useful system. I would only investigate whether an important handoff still requires repeated entry, manual reconciliation, or disconnected documents.

### “We do not have budget”

Ask:

- Is the problem not important enough?
- Is the timing wrong?
- Is the proposed scope too large?
- Is funding planned later?

Do not immediately discount.

### “Can you do a free audit first?”

> I can use the initial call to confirm whether a real issue exists. A documented workflow map, risk analysis, and implementation recommendation is paid work because it creates a usable decision deliverable. I can credit that fee toward the pilot.

### “Your price is high”

Ask:

- Compared with what alternative?
- Which deliverable appears misaligned?
- Is the concern total investment, timing, or uncertainty?

Then reduce scope before reducing value.

### “Send a proposal” before discovery

> I can send a useful proposal once the workflow, constraints, acceptance conditions, and decision owner are clear. Without that information, a fixed proposal would be unreliable.

### “We need it immediately”

Ask why. Identify:

- hard regulatory or contractual deadline;
- internal preference;
- emergency caused by a failed system.

Offer an emergency stabilization scope rather than promising the full solution instantly.

### “Can you guarantee ROI?”

> I can define measurable operational targets and acceptance criteria. A financial ROI guarantee would require reliable baseline data and control over factors beyond the software, so I will not promise a number that cannot be defended.

---

## 5.9 Red flags during calls

- disrespect toward previous contractors without facts;
- refusal to identify decision-makers;
- desire for unpaid prototype work;
- unwillingness to sign or pay a deposit;
- vague “huge future opportunity” used to justify low current payment;
- request to use unlicensed software or stolen data;
- impossible deadline with no scope reduction;
- refusal to provide data or access while expecting a fixed result;
- hidden stakeholders appearing late;
- demand for unlimited revisions;
- expectation of 24/7 availability without corresponding contract and price;
- insistence that you misrepresent project status.

Record red flags. Do not explain them away because you want revenue.

---

## 5.10 Closing the conversation

Every call ends with one of:

1. **No fit**  
   “Based on what we discussed, I do not think a custom project is justified.”

2. **Information action**  
   Each side receives a specific task and date.

3. **Future date**  
   Record an exact follow-up date and trigger.

4. **Paid mapping engagement**  
   Confirm deliverable, price, and proposal date.

5. **Pilot proposal**  
   Confirm scope basis, stakeholders, and delivery assumptions.

Never end with “Let’s stay in touch.”

---

Send within the same business day when possible.

```text

Hello [Name],

Thank you for the discussion. My understanding is:

- Current workflow: [brief summary]
- Main issue: [verified issue]
- Business consequence: [impact]
- Desired result: [target]
- Main constraints: [constraints]

Agreed next step:
- [You] will provide [item] by [date].
- [Client] will provide [item] by [date].

I will send [mapping offer/proposal] by [date].

Regards,
Anass
```

---

## 5.12 Sales-conversation maturity ladder

### Level 0

You improvise, over-explain technology, and leave without a next step.

### Level 1

Standard agenda, notes, qualification, and written recap.

### Level 2

Call types are separated; objection responses and stakeholder mapping are documented.

### Level 3

You measure call-to-offer and call-to-win conversion and review recordings/notes where legally and ethically appropriate.

### Level 4

Pre-call briefs and post-call summaries are assisted, while human listening and decisions remain central.

### Level 5

Your sector knowledge lets you diagnose patterns quickly without forcing assumptions, and buyers experience the conversation itself as valuable.

- reply-to-call rate;
- scheduled-to-attended rate;
- call-to-qualified-opportunity rate;
- call-to-paid-mapping rate;
- call-to-proposal rate;
- average days from first reply to call;
- average call length;
- no-fit rate;
- recurring objection categories;

# Guide 6 — Scope and sell projects

## 6.1 Purpose

Scoping converts an understood problem into a controlled commercial promise.

A scope must make clear:

- what will be delivered;
- what will not be delivered;
- how acceptance works;
- what the client must provide;
- how changes are handled;
- what it costs;
- when payments occur;
- what risks and assumptions exist.

A project is a temporary, structured effort intended to produce a defined outcome. [S14] Treat it as such; do not sell undefined “development help” without boundaries.

---

## 6.2 Offer architecture

### Offer 1 — Fit conversation

- free;
- 15–20 minutes;
- no detailed deliverable.

### Offer 2 — Intervention-to-Invoice Workflow Mapping

Initial price:

- €300–€500;
- 100% upfront;
- credited toward an accepted pilot within 30 days when commercially appropriate.

Deliverables:

- current-state workflow;
- systems and actors;
- duplicate-entry and delay points;
- data/document requirements;
- target-state workflow;
- recommended pilot;
- assumptions and risks;
- implementation range.

### Offer 3 — Micro-pilot

- €1,500–€3,500;
- one narrow workflow;
- limited users/integration complexity;
- written acceptance.

### Offer 4 — Core pilot

- €3,500–€7,500;
- multiple roles;
- one operational outcome;
- deployment, documentation, and stabilization.

### Offer 5 — Expansion

- phased;
- priced from evidence gathered in the pilot;
- never assumed before pilot acceptance.

### Offer 6 — Support

- explicit support scope;
- response windows;
- included hours/tasks;
- excluded project work;
- monthly or reserved-capacity pricing.

These are planning ranges, not universal market facts.

---

## 6.3 Mapping-engagement process

### Before sale

- confirm workflow owner;
- confirm participants;
- confirm access to documents/screens;
- confirm paid deliverable;
- send scope and invoice;
- receive payment.

### During mapping

1. Interview workflow owner.
2. Interview one representative field/operational user where possible.
3. Interview administrative or downstream user.
4. Review current forms, screenshots, or systems.
5. Draw current-state flow.
6. Identify failure modes and constraints.
7. Define target state.
8. Define smallest useful pilot.
9. Validate findings with client.

### Mapping deliverable structure

```markdown
# Workflow Mapping Brief

## Executive summary
## Business context
## Scope and exclusions
## Current-state workflow
## Roles and systems
## Information and documents
## Friction and risk register
## Root causes and uncertainties
## Target-state workflow
## Configure / integrate / build recommendation
## Recommended pilot
## Dependencies
## Acceptance measures
## Estimated investment and timeline
## Decision options
```

Do not hide uncertainty. List what must be validated during implementation.

---

## 6.4 Scope decomposition

Break a project into:

### Business outcomes

What operational result must exist?

### User roles

Who performs, approves, views, or administers work?

### Workflows

What sequence must the system support?

### Data

What records, fields, documents, and retention requirements exist?

### Integrations

What external systems and failure behavior exist?

### Non-functional requirements

- security;
- performance;
- availability;
- backup;
- recovery;
- auditability;
- accessibility;
- localization;
- maintainability.

### Deployment

- environment;
- hosting;
- domains;
- secrets;
- migration;
- rollback;
- monitoring.

### Acceptance

How will each deliverable be judged?

---

## 6.5 Requirements format

Use outcome-oriented requirements.

### Weak

> Build an advanced dashboard.

### Strong

> An operations coordinator can view all scheduled interventions for a selected day, filter by technician and status, and open the associated customer and equipment record.

### Acceptance criterion example

```text
Given an operations coordinator is authenticated,
when they select a date and technician,
then the system displays only interventions assigned to that technician on that date,
including customer, location, planned time, and current status.
```

### Requirement checklist

- actor;
- trigger;
- action;
- expected result;
- permission;
- validation;
- exception behavior;
- evidence of acceptance.

---

## 6.6 Assumptions and exclusions

### Assumptions

Examples:

- client provides valid API credentials;
- existing data is exportable;
- users have supported devices;
- one language is included;
- client responds to decisions within two business days;
- hosting supports required runtime.

### Exclusions

Examples:

- full accounting replacement;
- native mobile applications;
- historical data cleaning beyond agreed rules;
- third-party subscription fees;
- penetration test;
- 24/7 support;
- unlimited training;
- new features not listed in scope.

A clear exclusion is not negative. It protects the promise.

---

## 6.7 Estimation method

### Step 1 — Break down work

Create units small enough to reason about:

- discovery;
- design;
- data model;
- authentication;
- each workflow;
- integration;
- testing;
- deployment;
- documentation;
- training;
- stabilization.

### Step 2 — Estimate three values

For each unit:

- optimistic;
- most likely;
- pessimistic.

Use the pessimistic case to identify risk, not to hide enormous uncertainty.

### Step 3 — Add non-coding work

Include:

- meetings;
- communication;
- environment setup;
- testing;
- client delays;
- documentation;
- deployment;
- fixes;
- project administration.

### Step 4 — Add risk reserve

Risk reserve depends on:

- legacy code quality;
- third-party APIs;
- unclear data;
- external approvals;
- production criticality;
- access uncertainty.

### Step 5 — Validate against value and market

Price is not simply hours multiplied by an arbitrary low rate. But do not price far beyond your evidence and delivery maturity without justification.

---

## 6.8 Pricing structures

### Fixed price

Use when:

- scope is clear;
- acceptance is clear;
- dependencies are controlled.

### Paid discovery plus fixed price

Use when:

- problem is known;
- solution boundary is unclear;
- legacy system must be inspected.

### Time and materials with cap

Use when:

- work is investigative;
- priorities may change;
- a clear spending ceiling exists.

### Reserved capacity

Use after trust exists and the client has a continuing backlog.

### Avoid

- unlimited “monthly development” with no boundaries;
- fixed price for unknowable legacy work;
- pure hourly work without priorities, cap, or reporting;
- free discovery that produces substantial client value.

---

## 6.9 Payment terms

Planning structure:

### Below €500

- 100% upfront.

### €500–€2,000

- 50% upfront;
- 50% at staging acceptance.

### Above €2,000

- 40% upfront;
- 40% at working demonstration;
- 20% at final acceptance.

Rules:

- no work before cleared first payment;
- milestone dates do not override client dependencies;
- late payment pauses work under the contract;
- third-party fees are identified;
- taxes and bank/platform fees are handled explicitly;
- invoice and beneficiary identity must match your legal operating setup.

For Morocco-based service exports, confirm the current legal, tax, banking, and foreign-exchange requirements with a qualified Moroccan adviser. The Office des Changes’ 2026 instruction addresses service-export proceeds and repatriation requirements. [S8]

## 6.12 Change control

A change request is required when:

- a new user role appears;
- a new integration is added;
- data migration changes materially;
- acceptance behavior changes;
- a new environment is required;
- a new language or platform is added;
- client delay requires replanning;
- “small” feature requests accumulate.

### Change-request template

```markdown
# Change Request

- Project:
- Request date:
- Requested by:
- Description:
- Business reason:
- Scope affected:
- Timeline impact:
- Cost impact:
- Risk impact:
- Alternatives:
- Decision: Approved / Rejected / Deferred
- Approval names and dates:
```

No verbal change becomes committed work until recorded and approved.

---

## 6.13 Negotiation rules

### Do

- ask what concern drives the negotiation;
- reduce scope before reducing price;
- offer phased delivery;
- clarify value and risk controls;
- protect payment milestones;
- walk away from damaging terms.

### Do not

- discount immediately;
- apologize for charging;
- include unpriced extras;
- promise future discounts for vague referrals;
- accept payment entirely after completion;
- allow “unlimited revisions”;
- hide exclusions to win.

### Controlled alternatives

If budget is lower:

1. reduce roles;
2. remove integration;
3. reduce migration;
4. remove reporting;
5. use manual administration for the pilot;
6. postpone non-critical automation;
7. sell mapping first.

---

## 6.14 Proposal follow-up

When sending:

- schedule a review call;
- state proposal validity;
- identify decision-maker;
- identify decision date;
- list missing information.

Follow-up sequence:

1. Proposal review call.
2. Written clarification.
3. Decision-date reminder.
4. Close or move to future follow-up.

Do not chase indefinitely.

---

## 6.15 Scope-and-sales maturity ladder

### Level 0

Vague quotes and verbal promises.

### Level 1

Standard proposal, SOW, deposit, and acceptance criteria.

### Level 2

Paid discovery, risk register, change control, and milestone payments.

### Level 3

Estimation accuracy and margin measured; proposal library improves by project type.

### Level 4

Reusable productized components and standard engagement models reduce risk and sales time.

### Level 5

The business selectively prices for value, strategic proof, risk, capacity, and expansion potential while maintaining delivery discipline.

---

# Guide 7 — Deliver software

## 7.1 Purpose

Delivery is where trust becomes evidence.

The objective is not merely to finish code. It is to deliver an accepted operational result with controlled risk, recoverable deployment, clear ownership, and maintainable documentation.

Security should be integrated throughout the software development lifecycle rather than added after implementation. NIST’s Secure Software Development Framework and OWASP guidance both recommend embedding secure practices into the complete lifecycle. [S9][S15][S16]

---

## 7.2 Delivery principles

1. No implementation without agreed scope and initial payment.
2. No production change without backup and rollback.
3. No major feature without acceptance criteria.
4. No hidden project status.
5. No client secret in source control.
6. No unsupported claim of security or production readiness.
7. No out-of-scope request silently absorbed.
8. No handover without documentation.
9. No project closure without written acceptance or documented dispute.
10. No support obligation without written boundaries.

---

## 7.3 Delivery lifecycle

```text
Contract and deposit
→ kickoff
→ discovery confirmation
→ architecture and plan
→ implementation milestones
→ internal verification
→ client staging review
→ acceptance
→ production deployment
→ stabilization
→ handover
→ support/expansion decision
```

## 7.7 Architecture decision record

For important decisions, create an ADR.

```markdown
# ADR-[number]: [Decision]

## Status
Proposed / Accepted / Superseded

## Context
What problem or constraint requires a decision?

## Decision
What was chosen?

## Alternatives considered
What else was considered?

## Consequences
Benefits, costs, risks, and operational effects.

## Evidence/approval
Who reviewed and when?
```

Examples:

- integrate existing FSM versus custom module;
- PostgreSQL versus existing MySQL;
- queued versus synchronous external API calls;
- role-based permission model;
- document storage approach;
- deployment environment.

## 7.8 Project planning

You do not need to pretend a solo project is a full Scrum team. Use lightweight iterative delivery.

The Scrum Guide’s useful concepts include a clear Product Goal, Sprint Goal, and Definition of Done; adapt the concepts without mislabeling an incomplete partial process as full Scrum. [S17]

### Suggested milestone rhythm

- one-week milestones for small projects;
- one- or two-week increments for larger projects;
- working demonstration at each milestone;
- no milestone containing months of invisible work.

### Work-item template

```markdown
# Work Item

- Business outcome:
- User/role:
- Requirement:
- Acceptance criteria:
- Dependencies:
- Security/data considerations:
- Test plan:
- Demo evidence:
- Status:
```

### Definition of Ready

A work item is ready when:

- business purpose is clear;
- actor and behavior are clear;
- acceptance is clear;
- dependencies are available;
- major questions are resolved;
- scope fits the milestone.

### Definition of Done

A work item is done when:

- implementation complete;
- code reviewed by you against checklist;
- tests pass;
- security checks pass;
- migrations are safe;
- documentation updated;
- staging behavior demonstrated;
- acceptance evidence captured;
- no known critical defect remains.

---

## 7.9 Development environment

Minimum controls:

- version-controlled source;
- separate local, staging, and production configurations;
- secrets outside repository;
- reproducible setup instructions;
- dependency lock files;
- migration strategy;
- seed/demo data separated from client data;
- linting/static checks;
- automated tests;
- backup before destructive operations.

### Branch strategy for solo work

Simple option:

```text
main        production-ready accepted code
staging     integrated client-review code
feature/*   isolated work
hotfix/*    urgent production fix
```

Avoid long-lived branches and unreviewable giant commits.

### Commit standard

- small enough to understand;
- descriptive message;
- no secrets;
- tests included where relevant;
- migration and documentation changes committed with feature.

---

## 7.10 Secure development checklist

OWASP provides technology-neutral secure coding checklists covering validation, encoding, authentication, session management, access control, cryptography, error handling, data protection, communication, configuration, and database security. [S15]

### Input and output

- [ ] Server-side validation.
- [ ] Length and type limits.
- [ ] Allowlist where practical.
- [ ] Output encoding.
- [ ] Safe file handling.
- [ ] No trust in client-side validation alone.

### Authentication

- [ ] Reputable framework mechanisms.
- [ ] MFA where risk justifies it.
- [ ] Secure password storage.
- [ ] Rate limiting.
- [ ] Account recovery controls.
- [ ] No default credentials.

### Authorization

- [ ] Deny by default.
- [ ] Check permissions server-side.
- [ ] Test horizontal and vertical access.
- [ ] Separate administrative actions.
- [ ] Audit sensitive changes.

### Sessions

- [ ] Secure cookies.
- [ ] Appropriate expiry.
- [ ] Rotation after authentication.
- [ ] Logout invalidation.
- [ ] CSRF protection where relevant.

### Data protection

- [ ] Collect only needed data.
- [ ] Encrypt transport.
- [ ] Protect secrets.
- [ ] Avoid sensitive log content.
- [ ] Define retention and deletion.
- [ ] Secure backups.

### Errors and logging

- [ ] No stack traces to users in production.
- [ ] Correlation IDs where useful.
- [ ] Security-relevant events recorded.
- [ ] Log access controlled.
- [ ] Personal data minimized.
- [ ] Alerting for critical failures.

### Dependencies

- [ ] Pin versions.
- [ ] Review advisories.
- [ ] Remove unused packages.
- [ ] Document major third-party services.
- [ ] Verify licenses where necessary.

### Deployment

- [ ] Production debug disabled.
- [ ] Least-privilege service accounts.
- [ ] Security headers.
- [ ] TLS.
- [ ] Database not publicly exposed.
- [ ] Backup and restoration tested.

For higher-risk web applications, use OWASP ASVS as a verification requirements baseline. [S16]

## 7.11 Testing strategy

### Unit tests

Verify business rules and isolated behavior.

### Integration tests

Verify database, queues, APIs, and external adapters.

### Feature/end-to-end tests

Verify complete user workflows.

### Security tests

Verify:

- authentication;
- authorization;
- input handling;
- rate limits;
- file upload;
- sensitive actions;
- error behavior.

### Migration tests

Verify:

- forward migration;
- expected data transformation;
- rollback or recovery approach;
- behavior on realistic data volume.

### Acceptance tests

Directly map to the agreed acceptance criteria.

### Manual exploratory tests

Test unexpected sequences, error paths, and real user behavior.

### Test evidence

For each milestone, store:

- automated test output;
- screenshots or video;
- deployment identifier;
- acceptance checklist;
- known limitations.

- Accepted.
- Accepted with minor agreed defects.
- Rejected against a specific criterion.
- Change request.
- Deferred.

**Change:** requested behavior was not agreed or materially differs from scope.

## 7.16 Stabilization period

Define a fixed period, for example 7–30 days based on scope.

Includes:

- correction of in-scope defects;
- monitoring agreed critical workflows;
- minor documentation clarification.

Does not include:

- new features;
- changed business process;
- new integration;
- expanded user population;
- unrelated legacy defects;
- permanent support.

Create a support proposal before stabilization ends.

## 7.17 Handover

### Technical handover

- source code;
- repository ownership/access;
- setup instructions;
- architecture overview;
- environment inventory;
- deployment instructions;
- migration instructions;
- backup/restore instructions;
- monitoring/logging instructions;
- dependency list;
- known limitations;
- open risks.

### Operational handover

- user guide;
- administrator guide;
- role/permission explanation;
- support process;
- data export/deletion instructions;
- training recording or notes where agreed.

### Credentials

Do not send passwords in ordinary documents. Use a secure transfer process and require client-owned account recovery.

### Final handover acknowledgment

Obtain written acknowledgment that agreed materials were received.

## 7.18 Incident handling

When a production issue occurs:

1. Acknowledge quickly.
2. Determine severity.
3. Protect data and service.
4. Roll back or contain when necessary.
5. Communicate facts and uncertainty.
6. Record timeline.
7. Fix and verify.
8. Produce postmortem.
9. Prevent recurrence.

### Severity example

| Severity | Description | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Data loss, security incident, core service unavailable | Immediate containment and escalation |
| High | Major workflow unusable | Priority fix/workaround |
| Medium | Important defect with workaround | Scheduled correction |
| Low | Cosmetic/minor issue | Backlog |

Do not promise response times without a paid support agreement.

## 7.20 Delivery maturity ladder

### Level 0

Ad hoc coding and direct production edits.

### Level 1

Scope, Git, staging, tests, backup, and written acceptance.

### Level 2

Milestones, risk register, change control, secure checklist, deployment records, and handover.

### Level 3

Measured defects, estimate variance, lead time, deployment quality, and client satisfaction.

Reusable delivery automation, standardized environments, monitoring, and mature security verification.

### Level 5

Delivery consistently creates accepted outcomes, strong margin, reusable intellectual assets, referrals, and expansion—with low dependence on individual memory.

# Guide 8 — Convert completed work into more revenue

## 8.1 Purpose

A completed project should not become a dead archive.

A successful engagement can create:

1. final project revenue;
2. expansion revenue;
3. recurring support;
4. testimonial;
5. case study;
6. referral;
7. reusable components;
8. specialist knowledge;
9. stronger pricing;
10. easier future acquisition.

The compounding process must be systematic and ethical. Do not pressure a dissatisfied client for praise.

---

## 8.2 The post-delivery revenue sequence

```text
Acceptance
→ final payment
→ outcome review
→ stabilization
→ expansion diagnosis
→ support offer
→ testimonial permission
→ case-study permission
→ referral request
→ quarterly account review
```

Each step occurs only when the previous relationship supports it.

---

## 8.3 Final acceptance and payment

Before discussing expansion:

- [ ] Agreed deliverables accepted.
- [ ] Defects classified.
- [ ] Change requests separated.
- [ ] Handover delivered.
- [ ] Final invoice sent.
- [ ] Payment received or formal due date active.
- [ ] Client has an operational support route.

Do not blur final acceptance with new sales pressure.

---

## 8.4 Outcome review

Schedule a 30–45-minute review after the system has been used long enough to produce evidence.

Questions:

- What changed in the workflow?
- Which users adopted it?
- What still requires manual work?
- What became faster or clearer?
- What defects or friction remain?
- Did the original business consequence improve?
- Which metric can be compared with baseline?
- What should be improved next?

### Evidence to collect

- time from intervention completion to administrative validation;
- number of duplicate entries;
- missing-document rate;
- invoice-preparation delay;
- number of tools used;
- support requests;
- user adoption;
- error/rework count;
- qualitative client statements.

Do not invent a baseline after delivery. Establish measurement before or during the project when possible.

## 8.5 Expansion discovery

Expansion should follow the operational chain.

Example:

```text
Pilot: digital intervention report
→ Expansion 1: parts consumption
→ Expansion 2: office validation
→ Expansion 3: invoice integration
→ Expansion 4: customer portal
→ Expansion 5: management reporting
```

### Expansion questions

- Which adjacent step still breaks the workflow?
- Does another branch need the same capability?
- Are more roles required?
- Is data still re-entered elsewhere?
- Is reporting now the limiting problem?
- Does another system need integration?
- Does historical data need migration?

### Expansion quality gate

Do not sell expansion merely because you can build it.

Require:

- verified need;
- owner;
- business consequence;
- adoption of current system;
- stable foundation;
- funding;
- new scope and acceptance.

## 8.6 Support product design

### Basic maintenance

Possible inclusion:

- dependency/security updates;
- backup monitoring;
- uptime/health monitoring;
- limited bug-resolution allowance;
- monthly status.

### Maintenance plus improvement

Possible inclusion:

- basic maintenance;
- small monthly improvement allowance;
- prioritized backlog;
- monthly review.

### Reserved capacity

Possible inclusion:

- defined number of days/hours;
- priority scheduling;
- planned development;
- operational review.

### Every support agreement must define

- covered systems;
- covered environments;
- response windows;
- business hours;
- included effort;
- excluded work;
- incident severity;
- backup responsibility;
- third-party responsibility;
- payment timing;
- rollover rules;
- termination.

### Do not include by default

- 24/7 response;
- unlimited changes;
- unlimited users/training;
- third-party outages;
- new projects;
- emergency work unrelated to your delivery.

## 8.7 Testimonial process

Request only after a positive accepted outcome.

### Ask for specific truth

```text
Hello [Name],

Thank you again for the collaboration. Would you be comfortable providing a short, accurate testimonial covering:

- what you asked me to deliver;
- how the delivery and communication went;
- what changed or became easier after implementation?

I can draft a factual version from our project summary for you to edit and approve. I will publish nothing without your approval.
```

### Testimonial record

Store:

- exact approved wording;
- person name;
- role;
- company;
- approval date;
- permitted channels;
- whether logo/photo may be used;
- expiry or review condition.

Do not rewrite a modest statement into an exaggerated outcome.

## 8.8 Case-study permission

Separate testimonial permission from case-study permission.

Confirm:

- company name use;
- logo use;
- project description;
- screenshots;
- architecture;
- metrics;
- confidential exclusions;
- review before publication;
- public repository status.

### Sanitized case study

When public naming is not permitted:

- describe sector and company size generally;
- remove identifying data;
- use synthetic screenshots;
- label them clearly;
- preserve truthful project classification.

## 8.9 Referral process

Ask after value has been delivered, not immediately after signing.

### Specific referral request

```text
I am focusing on companies with field teams where intervention information, parts, documents, and invoicing cross several tools. If you know one business facing a similar workflow problem, would you be comfortable introducing us? A simple email introduction is enough; I will handle it professionally and will not pressure them.
```

### Referral rules

- make the target clear;
- make the introduction easy;
- do not demand a list of contacts;
- do not use the client’s name without permission;
- thank the introducer;
- keep them informed appropriately;
- never damage their reputation through spammy behavior.

# Part V — Decision rules that protect the business

- professional relevance is absent;
- identity or domain is uncertain;
- contact source is unsupported;

- acceptance is unclear;

- agreement is unsigned;
- deposit is unpaid;
- client owner is missing;
- access is missing;

- tests fail;
- backup is unverified;
- rollback is unavailable;
- exact version is unknown;

- written acceptance or documented dispute;
- final invoice;

- known limitations;

# Final operating principle

Your work is not:

```text
Find lead → send message → write code
```

Your complete business is:

```text
Build credible evidence
→ research the right organizations
→ qualify without pretending to know their problem
→ contact the correct professional respectfully
→ investigate the real workflow
→ sell a bounded and controlled promise
→ deliver secure, accepted software
→ turn the result into expansion, support, proof, and referrals
→ improve the system using measured evidence
```

Keep the system lean until repeated bottlenecks justify new tools. Keep human judgment at every high-risk commercial, compliance, and delivery boundary. Automate repetition only after the manual process is correct.
